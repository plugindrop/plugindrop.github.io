## Why "Just Add Width" Doesn't Work

The instinct when a mix sounds narrow is to reach for a widener and push it up. The problem: indiscriminate widening creates phase issues and mixes that sound wide on headphones but collapse on mono playback — smart speakers, phone speakers, club systems in mono-check mode, and a significant chunk of streaming.

Stereo width is most effective as **contrast**. A mix where the low end is tightly centered and width is applied selectively to high-frequency content, pads, and reverb tails sounds immersive precisely because those wide elements have a focused center to push against. When everything is widened, the contrast disappears — and with it, the punchy, forward center image.

The correct workflow:

1. Check mono compatibility before adding anything
2. Use mid/side EQ to clean up the existing stereo field
3. Apply stereo imaging to specific elements — not the whole mix
4. Use the Haas effect sparingly, for creative effect only

---

## Step 1: Check Mono Compatibility First (Free)

Before touching any widening plugin, load **SPAN** (free, Voxengo) on your master bus and hit the mono button on your DAW. Watch two things:

- **Phase correlation meter** — anything below zero means you have phase cancellation. Mono playback will literally remove energy from your mix.
- **Low-frequency energy** — your kick and bass should not change significantly when you flip to mono. If they thin out, your low end has stereo content it shouldn't have.

SPAN costs nothing and will immediately tell you if you have a problem. Fix mono issues *before* adding any width. A common source: stereo reverb on bass, a stereo synth with wide unison spread placed below 150Hz, or a sample that was recorded or rendered with accidental stereo processing.

---

## Step 2: Mid/Side EQ — The Surgical Approach

The most controlled way to add stereo width is mid/side EQ, because you're not widening anything — you're selectively boosting or cutting energy in the side channel at specific frequencies. This is precise, phase-coherent, and easy to undo.

**FabFilter Pro-Q 4** ($199) is the best option here. Its M/S mode lets you place individual EQ bands on the mid channel, the side channel, or both, with visual feedback showing exactly where energy lives in the stereo field.

Practical M/S EQ moves that actually work:

- **High-shelf boost on the side channel (above 8–10kHz):** Adds air and width to the high end without touching the midrange. Start at +2dB and listen on speakers before going further.
- **Low-cut on the side channel (below 150–200Hz):** Cleans up mono compatibility issues immediately. Almost every mix benefits from this.
- **Narrow notch cut on the mid channel at a harsh frequency:** Removes harshness from the center without touching the wider elements that might not share that frequency.

Pro-Q 4 at $199 is a significant buy, but it's FabFilter's flagship and goes on sale regularly. See [when FabFilter Pro-Q 4 goes on sale](/blog/when-does-fabfilter-pro-q-4-go-on-sale) before buying at full price.

## Step 3: Imaging Plugins — Controlled Widening

Once your mono compatibility is solid, imaging plugins add genuine width without the phase chaos of simple mid/side manipulation.

### iZotope Ozone Imager (Free)

The standalone Ozone Imager is free and genuinely useful. It shows a Lissajous/vectorscope display (so you can see phase correlation in real time) and lets you widen or narrow the stereo field with a single knob. The **Stereoize** mode creates stereo width from mono content — useful on a mono room mic or synth pad.

Use it on individual channels, not the master bus. It's excellent on:
- Pad layers and string synths
- Parallel reverb returns
- Room mic buses
- Overhead buses on drum tracks

### NUGEN Audio Stereoizer ($99)

Where Ozone Imager is a quick-and-clean option, NUGEN Stereoizer is built for transparent source widening. It uses psychoacoustic processing to create width that survives mono fold-down better than most alternatives. At $99 it's a fair buy for producers who regularly work with mono sources (vocals, guitars recorded center) and need to place them in the stereo field without obvious artifacts.

### iZotope Ozone 11 Standard ($249 / often $99 on sale)

The full Ozone 11 suite includes a **multiband Imager** — you can widen the high frequencies independently of the mids, or tighten the low end while keeping the top end open. For mastering or mix-bus use, this is meaningfully more powerful than the free Imager. Ozone goes on sale frequently; check [when iZotope Ozone goes on sale](/blog/when-does-izotope-ozone-go-on-sale) — you should rarely pay full price.

## Step 4: The Haas Effect (Use Carefully)

The Haas effect — delaying the same audio signal 10–40ms on one side — creates a strong perception of width. The catch: it creates genuine phase issues. Summed to mono, the delayed duplicate partially cancels with the original.

When to use it:
- **Pads and atmospheric synths** where mono cancellation is acceptable or even desirable for the genre
- **Creative effects** — not as a default width tool

When not to use it:
- Lead synths or anything that needs to cut through on a mono system
- Any element where consistent presence across playback systems matters

If you use Haas, always check mono. If the element disappears or changes character significantly in mono, use mid/side EQ or an imaging plugin instead.

---

## The Correct Order of Operations

1. **Analyze:** SPAN on master, check phase correlation and mono
2. **Fix:** Remove stereo content from bass frequencies (M/S EQ low-cut on side)
3. **Shape:** M/S EQ on individual channels or buses to place elements in the field
4. **Widen selectively:** Ozone Imager on pads, reverb returns, overheads — not the whole mix
5. **Check:** Flip to mono again. Nothing important should disappear.
