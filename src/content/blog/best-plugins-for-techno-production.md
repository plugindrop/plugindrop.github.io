## FabFilter Pro-Q 4

<!-- role:pick -->

<!-- pick:FabFilter Pro-Q 4 -->

FabFilter Pro-Q 4 is an equalizer plug-in offering up to 24 bands of processing, built around a standout addition: Spectral dynamics, which treats problem frequencies with more precision and efficiency than earlier approaches. This makes it well suited to mixing and mastering engineers working across stereo, surround, or Dolby Atmos sessions. The display adapts to the task at hand — 3 dB and 6 dB ranges are tailored for the fine detail mastering work demands, while 12 dB and 30 dB ranges give mixing engineers the wider view they need when shaping individual tracks.

Beyond Spectral dynamics, the plug-in's Dynamic EQ mode lets individual bands respond to signal level, with Attack and Release controls and an optional free side-chain filter for triggering bands from a different frequency range than the one being processed. The dynamic EQ processing itself has been refined to produce less distortion and a cleaner overall sound, and this improvement carries across all of its operating modes: zero latency, Natural Phase, and linear phase. For producers comparing tools in this space, Pro-Q 4 sits alongside other well-regarded EQs such as BALANCE, Eventide SplitEQ, and SSL Native X-EQ 2.

## FabFilter Pro-C 3

<!-- role:pick -->

<!-- pick:FabFilter Pro-C 3 -->

FabFilter Pro-C 3 is a high-end stereo and surround compressor with fourteen compressor styles organized into Modern, Classic, and Utility categories. Its standout feature is combined upward and downward multiband dynamics processing through FabFilter's TTM technology, letting producers shape loudness and density in ways a standard compressor cannot. The plugin covers an unusually wide range of use cases, from transparent mastering control to punchy mixbus glue, classic opto-style warmth, modern upward dynamics, and aggressive pumping effects. On the character side, modes built around Tube, Diode, and Bright settings add analog-style saturation, coloring, and drift, giving engineers a way to push the sound beyond clean compression when a track calls for more texture. The transparent mastering mode keeps gain reduction tightly focused for situations where control matters more than character, while the punchier and more aggressive modes are suited to drum bus work or full mixes that need extra impact and glue. Overall, the plugin positions itself as flexible enough to handle delicate mastering-stage work and bold, characterful mixing decisions within the same tool.

## Soundtoys Decapitator

<!-- role:pick -->

<!-- pick:Soundtoys Decapitator -->

Decapitator is a saturation plugin built for the kind of controlled distortion techno often calls for — not the obvious clipping of a guitar amplifier, but the subtle harmonic coloration that makes synthesizers and drum machines sound as if they were run through real hardware. It models five distinct analog saturation circuits, each drawn from a different piece of classic studio gear, with each one adding its own frequency character. Its Drive control covers a wide range, from a light touch of warmth up to outright distortion, making it capable of anything from giving a subtractive synth the sound of passing through a console channel strip to producing aggressive, industrial-style textures. It's available as a VST3, AU, or AAX plugin for both Windows and macOS, and it suits producers who want adjustable, analog-style saturation and distortion on synth and drum channels, spanning subtle warmth through to harsher, more aggressive coloration.

## u-he Diva

<!-- role:pick -->

<!-- pick:u-he Diva -->

u-he Diva remains one of the most convincing analog synth emulations available, built to recreate classic hardware synthesizers such as the Minimoog, Roland Jupiter-8, Juno-60, Korg MS-20, and Oberheim SEM. Rather than approximating how those instruments behaved, Diva models their circuits directly, which is why its filter self-oscillation, oscillator drift, and subtle tuning imperfections come through as genuinely authentic, unrivalled analogue sound rather than a digital imitation. For classic Detroit-style techno, where the signature tone comes from real Junos and Prophets run through hardware effects, Diva is a software option built to capture that source material rather than simply approximate it.

The plugin includes an even bigger library of organic presets than earlier versions, and its editor window can be resized to fit different studio setups, with alternative skins planned for the future. Diva has always been CPU-heavy, though the current version cuts CPU usage by up to 40 percent compared with the original release and adds multithreading support so modern multi-core processors can run more voices without overloading. Producers working through demanding sessions can still freeze tracks or run Diva in a lower-quality mode while tracking, then switch to full quality for the final bounce.

Diva runs on Mac OS X 10.9 or newer, including Apple Silicon M1 Macs, and on Windows 7 or newer, and needs at least 1GB of RAM, with more recommended. It suits producers chasing authentic vintage analog synthesis — Detroit-style pads, Roland-style bass sequences, Minimoog-style leads — who want the character of several vintage synths bundled into one instrument, alongside alternatives like Reveal Sound Spire, Analog Polysynth Collection, or Arturia Analog Lab Pro.

## How to choose

<!-- role:choose -->

Producers choosing among these plugins should base the decision on what stage their track is at and what kind of sound they're after. For Berlin-style techno built on acid elements, Serum is the stronger starting synthesizer, since its modulation system and wavetable editing suit acid lines and industrial leads. For long, slowly evolving arrangements, Arturia Pigments is the better pick: its granular and layered synthesis engines are built for textures that hold up across extended club edits.

For mixing aimed at club playback, Pro-Q 4 and Pro-C 3 are the pair most likely to matter at high volume, and are worth prioritizing before adding more synthesizers. Pro-Q 4 offers up to 24 bands along with dynamic EQ, where individual bands respond to the signal's own level rather than applying a constant cut or boost, plus attack and release controls and optional free side-chain filtering, with a choice of zero-latency, natural-phase, or linear-phase processing — making it suited to stereo, surround, or Dolby Atmos sessions in both mixing and mastering. Pro-C 3 spans fourteen compressor styles across modern, classic, and utility categories and combines upward and downward multiband dynamics, so it can deliver transparent mastering control, punchy mixbus glue, classic opto-style warmth, or aggressive pumping, with character modes adding analog-style coloring.

For reverb, one option leans toward dark, club-style spaces, while the other leans toward industrial and vintage-leaning textures; many producers working seriously in the genre end up using both rather than settling on just one.

If a synthesizer sound feels too clean or digital, u-he Diva is worth considering, but only when the target is a vintage analog character. Diva aims for an authentic analog sound, uses multithreading to support more polyphony with less strain on the processor, runs with noticeably lower CPU demand than its earlier version, and includes a large library of organic-sounding presets in a resizable editor window. For deliberately digital textures, Serum or Pigments remain the better direction.

## FAQ

<!-- role:faq -->

**Q: Do I need hardware synthesizers to produce techno in 2026?**
A: No. Software synthesizers — wavetable instruments for acid sound design and component-level analog emulations such as Diva — cover the range of synthesizer textures that define the genre. Most professional techno released in 2026 is made in software. Hardware mainly shapes workflow and adds subtle sonic character; it does not produce results that software cannot match at this level.

**Q: What is the most important single investment for techno production?**
A: A wavetable synthesizer such as Serum is the priority for producers still building core sound design skills. A dynamic equalizer such as Pro-Q 4 is the priority for producers who already have strong synthesizers but need their mixes to translate on club systems. Which one matters more depends on whether the current limitation is sound design or mix quality.

**Q: Can I make professional techno with free plugins?**
A: Yes. Free synthesizers can cover a meaningful portion of the synthesis capability found in paid wavetable synths, and free reverb and EQ plugins provide capable processing. Paid plugins add a specific sonic character and workflow efficiency that matters for professional-level output, but skill level affects results more than plugin choice at any budget.

**Q: How do I make techno kicks translate on club systems?**
A: The kick processing chain matters more than any single plugin. Start with a well-designed sample or synthesized layer, use an equalizer such as Pro-Q 4 to focus on a specific low-frequency range around the fundamental (typically 60 to 80 Hz for Berlin-style techno), apply bus compression such as Pro-C 3 to the drum group, and check the mix at high volume on full-range speakers before finalizing. Low-frequency decisions made on headphones or small monitors do not translate accurately to club systems.

**Q: What reverb approach works best for techno?**
A: Keep reverb on percussion subtle and weighted toward low frequencies; dark, short-decay settings prevent the reverb from washing out the rhythm. Use longer reverb tails on synthesizer parts rather than on kick drums to add depth. Pre-delay on synthesizer reverb creates separation between the dry sound and the reverb tail, which keeps a dense mix clear. A late-reflections control can be used to manage this separation directly.

**Q: Is a CPU-heavy, component-level analog synth such as Diva worth the overhead?**
A: Yes, if the target sound is vintage analog techno. If the production style is modern and digital, the extra CPU cost is not justified, since other synthesis approaches deliver better results for those styles at lower cost. The specific value of a component-level analog emulation is producing sounds that are difficult to distinguish from hardware analog synthesizers, which is a stylistic choice rather than a universal upgrade.
