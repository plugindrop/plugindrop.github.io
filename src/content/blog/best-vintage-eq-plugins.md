FabFilter Pro-Q 4 is a precision equalizer plug-in rather than a vintage hardware replica — it doesn't model any specific SSL, Neve, or API unit. It earns a place alongside dedicated console emulations because it offers a different kind of control: full parametric precision instead of hardware-specific character. The plug-in supports up to 24 bands, and its Natural Phase mode is designed to reproduce the frequency-dependent phase behavior found in analog filters. An optional Analog output stage adds saturation that shifts how the plug-in behaves at higher gain settings.

Pro-Q 4 also includes Spectral dynamics, which targets problem frequencies more precisely and efficiently than standard dynamic EQ, along with a broader dynamic EQ feature set offering adjustable attack and release times and optional free side-chain filtering. These dynamic controls adjust band gain based on signal level at each frequency, giving flexibility that a fixed-topology vintage emulation can't match. Processing across zero-latency, Natural Phase, and linear-phase modes has been refined to produce less distortion and a cleaner overall sound.

## FAQ

<!-- role:faq -->

Q: What makes a vintage EQ emulation accurate versus a generic "analog-sounding" plugin?
A: An accurate emulation models specific circuit behavior, including transformer saturation, inductor-based frequency curves, and how gain stages respond to changes in signal level. A generic analog mode typically just adds soft clipping or slightly rounded filter shapes without reproducing that underlying circuit behavior. Technical documentation describing the modeling approach behind a plugin is the first thing worth checking when evaluating a claim like this.

Q: What is the practical difference between an SSL and a Neve EQ?
A: SSL 4000-series EQs are generally described as fast, punchy, and relatively transparent at moderate gain, which is why they tend to get used on drums, modern pop, and high-output mixes. Neve 1073-style EQs are generally described as warmer and more colored, a result of their transformer-heavy circuit design, which is why they tend to get used for rock, R&B, and vintage-style warmth. These differences come from the hardware design itself rather than from how a product is positioned or marketed.

Q: Does a precision EQ plugin that doesn't model a specific hardware unit still count as a vintage EQ emulation?
A: Not in the traditional sense. A plugin like this does not replicate one particular piece of hardware. What it offers instead is analog-style filter behavior, often paired with an optional analog-style output stage. It suits precision work such as corrective EQ, surgical cuts, and mastering, where exact vintage character matters less than fine control. It is best thought of as a complement to dedicated vintage emulations rather than a replacement for them.

Q: Are free Pultec-style emulations good enough for professional use?
A: For the core passive EQ behavior — the simultaneous boost and attenuation at the same frequency that creates the distinctive Pultec low-end — free emulations can capture that foundational behavior. Commercial emulations from established plugin developers typically add further circuit detail on top of that.

Q: Which plugin format should be prioritized currently?
A: VST3 and AU are the current standards: VST3 for Windows digital audio workstations and AU for Mac-based ones, while AAX is required for Pro Tools. Support for the older VST2 format is being phased out by most major developers. Plugins aiming for broad compatibility typically ship with VST3, AU, and AAX as the standard package.
