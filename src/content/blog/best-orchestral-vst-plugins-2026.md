## How to choose

<!-- role:choose -->

Choosing the right orchestral library mostly comes down to how someone works and what the music needs to do, rather than any single "best" option.

For producers who want one library that covers every orchestral section with a consistent overall sound, a library recorded as a complete ensemble in one room tends to work better than sections pieced together from separate sessions. Spitfire Audio BBC Symphony Orchestra fits that description, since strings, brass, and woodwinds were captured together rather than assembled from different recordings.

For those who need to sketch cinematic ideas quickly and don't want to program individual keyswitches or articulations by hand, a plugin built around chord and rhythm engines is the more practical choice, since those engines can turn a simple idea into a playable arrangement within minutes. Sonuscore's The Orchestra Complete 2 is built around that kind of fast-sketching workflow.

Producers layering strings into pop, hip-hop, or R&B tracks generally benefit from a library that loads quickly and sits comfortably in a mix without a lot of additional adjustment. Native Instruments Session Strings Pro 2 is suited to that kind of use, trading some of the deeper articulation control found in larger libraries for speed and a smaller footprint.

When computer resources or disk space are limited, it makes more sense to start with free libraries before committing to a large paid one that can take up several gigabytes. Spitfire Audio LABS and ProjectSAM Free Orchestra are both free and worth trying first, with LABS offering a rotating set of patches and ProjectSAM Free Orchestra offering a more complete standing set covering strings, brass, woodwinds, and percussion.

Finally, for situations where articulation accuracy matters more than speed, libraries built for composers who program detailed performances by hand are the better fit. Orchestral Tools Berlin Strings and VSL Synchron Strings Pro were both designed with that kind of granular, hand-programmed control in mind, offering detailed bowing, dynamics, and round-robin options rather than one-touch playback shortcuts.
