## Introduction

<!-- role:intro -->

A common misunderstanding about piano VST plugins is that a bigger sample library automatically means a more realistic sound. Pianoteq 8 pushes back on that idea: it uses no recorded samples at all, generating its piano sound through physical modeling instead, and the whole plugin installs in roughly 80MB — a tiny footprint next to sample-based libraries that can run into many gigabytes. That gap shows that a plugin's size and how convincing it sounds are two different things. No listening tests were carried out for this guide, so the plugins here are not ranked against one another on sound quality.

Piano plugins generally fall into a few broad types. Sample-based libraries record a real piano note by note and play those recordings back. Physical modeling instruments work differently: instead of relying on samples, they calculate the sound of a piano from scratch, which keeps the install size small and lets players adjust details of how the instrument behaves — things like hammer action, string age, unison detuning, stretch tuning, lid position, and the resonance of the soundboard and strings.

Mechanical details such as pedal noise, key noise, and hammer noise can often be shaped as well, and many of these instruments offer several microphone positions to change the character of the recorded tone. Beyond modeled and sampled acoustic pianos, other categories cover electric piano and keyboard emulations, along with hybrid instruments that blend recorded samples with modeled resonance and other processing.

- **Formats:** VST, AAX, Audio Unit, NKS (64-bit DAWs only)

Arturia Piano V3 runs on an advanced physical modeling engine that generates its piano sounds entirely through calculation rather than relying on recorded samples. The plugin requires a 64-bit system: Windows 10 or later, or macOS 10.13 or later with an Apple Silicon or capable Intel processor, along with at least 4 GB of RAM and 3 GB of free disk space.

## Pianoteq 8 (Modartt)

<!-- role:pick -->

<!-- pick:Pianoteq 8 (Modartt) -->

Pianoteq 8 (Modartt) — the physical modeling benchmark

- **Developer:** Modartt

- **Platforms:** Windows, macOS, Linux

- **Formats:** VST3, AU, AAX

Pianoteq 8 contains no sample recordings. Every note is generated in real time through physics simulation of string tension, hammer hardness, soundboard resonance, and room interaction. The resulting install size is approximately 80MB. The engine handles sympathetic resonance and velocity-dependent timbral shifts in real time rather than through fixed sample layers. Instrument add-ons (including licensed models of specific high-end grands) are available as paid extensions. The one honest trade-off: Pianoteq has a slightly synthesized quality on isolated slow lines that disappears entirely in a full production mix.

**Best for:** Producers on limited storage, live performers, and anyone who needs true physical behavior across the full dynamic range.

## How to choose

<!-- role:choose -->

For someone starting from zero who wants an acoustic grand sound, downloading Piano One at no cost is the sensible first move. It's capable enough for demo work, and it will make clear what's missing once the time comes to invest in a paid instrument.

For producers after atmospheric, non-traditional piano textures, Spitfire LABS Soft Piano is a solid free starting point, with room to move up to the full Felt Piano once its single, fixed tonal character starts to feel limiting.

For anyone who wants several distinct piano types without having to manage separate libraries, Arturia Piano V3 is the straightforward pick. It pairs a physical modeling engine with multiple piano types in one plugin, at a reasonable price, and its four macro controls make shaping the sound quick rather than fiddly.

For those constrained by storage space, or who perform live, Pianoteq 8's sample-free engine runs on almost any machine without a large install, and handles extreme dynamic playing more convincingly than instruments built from fixed sample layers.

For producers working in R&B, soul, or neo-soul who need an electric piano, Scarbee Mark I offers a vintage Rhodes character baked right into its recordings, while Lounge Lizard EP-4 suits anyone who would rather shape the electric piano tone themselves instead of working from a preset sound.

For anyone with no budget constraints who wants acoustic and electric pianos covered under one license, Keyscape is the only plugin in this group that handles both at a professional standard without requiring separate purchases.

## FAQ

<!-- role:faq -->

Q: What is the best free piano VST plugin in 2026?
A: Spitfire LABS Soft Piano is a good choice for textured, atmospheric work, while Piano One offers a neutral acoustic grand sound. Both plugins are free and cover different tonal territory, so downloading both costs nothing and takes only a few minutes.

Q: Sampled piano or modeled piano — which sounds more realistic?
A: The answer depends on the situation. Sampled pianos such as Keyscape, Ravenscroft 275, and The Grandeur play back the exact tone of one specific recorded instrument, which tends to sound most realistic for exposed solo piano parts. Modeled pianos such as Pianoteq 8 and Lounge Lizard EP-4 generate sound in real time using physics simulation, so they handle continuous dynamics, pedal behavior, and sympathetic resonance more naturally, and they install using far less storage space. For solo classical or jazz playing, sampled instruments generally sound slightly more convincing; for live performance, expressive dynamic playing, and situations where install size matters, modeled instruments tend to be the stronger option.

Q: Is Pianoteq worth buying instead of a sample-based piano library?
A: For people who perform live, work on computers with limited storage space, or write music where the piano sits alongside many other instruments, the physical modeling approach is often the better fit, since it handles nuance at extreme playing dynamics more effectively than fixed sample layers can. For solo classical or jazz piano where raw realism matters most, a large sample library such as Keyscape or Ravenscroft 275 is usually the more convincing choice on its own.

Q: Can these piano plugins run in any recording program?
A: Most of these plugins support the VST3, AU, and AAX plugin formats, which cover the major recording programs, including Ableton Live, Logic Pro, FL Studio, Cubase, Pro Tools, and Studio One. Some instruments, such as The Grandeur, Scarbee Mark I, and Una Corda, require a free sample-player program to run. Other instruments built on the LABS platform require their own free player program as well. Neither of these free players adds any extra cost.

Q: What is the difference between a felt piano and a toy piano?
A: A felt piano is a regular grand or upright piano with felt strips placed between the hammers and strings, which creates a dry, muted, intimate tone. This is the same method used when a piano is set up for quiet practice. A toy piano, on the other hand, is a separate, smaller instrument that uses metal rods or tines instead of strings, producing a thinner, more metallic, high-pitched sound. One instrument on this list, Una Corda, sits between both categories because it was recorded as a prepared piano rather than built purely around either single technique.

Q: How much computer processing power and memory do piano plugins need?
A: This depends on how the sound is generated. Large sample-based libraries such as Keyscape, which can take up more than seventy gigabytes of storage, rely on reading data from a hard drive and work best with a solid-state drive and at least sixteen gigabytes of memory. Instruments built on physical modeling, such as Pianoteq 8, install in under one hundred megabytes and use very little memory, but they rely more heavily on processing power to generate sound in real time. A single piano instrument running by itself is usually light on system resources; the strain tends to show up only when many sample-based instruments are running together in one project.

Q: Does getting the most out of these plugins require an expensive keyboard controller?
A: The only feature that really matters across all of these plugins is velocity sensitivity, meaning the keyboard can sense how hard each key is pressed. Any keyboard controller with velocity-sensitive keys will work well. A small number of articulations in Pianoteq and Keyscape make use of aftertouch, which senses pressure held after a key is pressed, but this is not required for ordinary piano playing.
