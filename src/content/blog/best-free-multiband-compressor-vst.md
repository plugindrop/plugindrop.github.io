## Introduction

<!-- role:intro -->

Multiband compression has become a popular tool for home studio producers, and in 2026 free plugins can do much of what pricier options offer. TDR Nova stands out as a free multiband compressor suited to both mixing and mastering, working as a kind of hybrid between a dynamic equalizer and a compressor. OTT, made by Xfer Records, is another well-known free multiband compressor, built with EDM sound design and bus compression in mind. For producers who want more depth and finer control over individual frequency bands, MMultiband, part of MeldaProduction's free plugin bundle, covers the rest of what these other tools don't.

OTT in particular has become one of the most recognizable free multiband compressors around, even though it wasn't made by a major plugin company. It's a three-band compressor that pushes both upward and downward, built around essentially a single main control knob, and it originated as a preset from an Ableton default effects rack. That simplicity — one knob doing most of the work — is a big part of why it's so widely used. Taken together, the free multiband compressor plugins available in 2026 can match much of what paid plugins in the higher price range provide.

For a bedroom producer, multiband compression becomes useful once single-band compression starts creating problems rather than solving them: a muddy low end can trigger gain reduction in the high-mid frequencies, harsh high frequencies can't be tamed without also losing warmth elsewhere, or separate frequency zones on a group bus each need their own level of density and punch. Used carelessly, though, multiband compression can introduce pumping and sound unnatural rather than musical.

**Q: What is a multiband compressor and when should I use one?**

A: A multiband compressor splits audio into frequency bands and applies independent compression to each one. Use it when a single-band compressor creates more problems than it solves — for example, when heavy bass is triggering gain reduction that affects the high-mids, or when a specific frequency range needs to be tamed without touching the rest of the spectrum. For straightforward dynamics control without frequency interaction issues, a standard single-band compressor is usually simpler and more appropriate.

**Q: Is TDR Nova a multiband compressor or a dynamic EQ?**

A: Both — and the practical distinction is less important than it used to be. TDR Nova can operate as a dynamic EQ, a parallel compressor, or a traditional multiband compressor depending on how its bands are configured. Developer documentation explicitly positions it as a hybrid tool, so it can be used for all three functions depending on the session.

**Q: Why is OTT so popular in EDM production specifically?**

A: OTT applies upward compression (raising quiet signal) and downward compression (reducing loud signal) simultaneously across three frequency bands. The result is an aggressive, hyper-compressed tonal density typical of the "in-your-face" character of modern electronic music. Its zero-cost availability and near-zero learning curve account for the rest of its reach — it delivers a specific, recognizable result immediately.

**Q: Are free multiband compressors usable on professional and commercial releases?**

A: Yes, for most standard compression tasks. TDR Nova and Waves C6 Lite both offer serious multiband control. The meaningful advantages of paid tools are workflow speed, advanced phase processing modes, and deeper metering — not a fundamental quality difference for typical use cases.

**Q: What is the difference between a multiband compressor and a multiband limiter?**

A: A compressor reduces gain above a threshold at a configurable ratio; a limiter uses an extreme ratio — typically 10:1 or higher — to hard-cap signal from exceeding a ceiling. Some mastering tools like iZotope Ozone 12 combine both in an integrated processing chain. For mixing and dynamics shaping, a multiband compressor is the appropriate tool; for final output ceiling control before delivery, a limiter is used after the compressor stage.

**Q: What's the best free multiband compressor for mastering?**

A: Based on specs, TDR Nova is the free option here with the mastering-oriented features — its linear-phase mode and mid/side processing address the phase-coherence and stereo-control demands that mastering places on a compressor. Waves C6 Lite is an option when you want six discrete bands, and MMultiband is for deeper per-band control. For a fully integrated mastering chain that also handles limiting, imaging, and reference metering, a paid suite like iZotope Ozone 12 remains a step above any free standalone compressor.

**Q: Do multiband compressors use a lot of CPU?**

A: It depends on the design. Crossover-based multiband compressors running linear-phase modes — including TDR Nova in linear phase and MMultiband with high-quality oversampling — cost more CPU because they add latency and extra processing per band. Check each plugin's system requirements on the developer's page for CPU needs. If CPU headroom is tight, a practical approach is to reserve linear-phase modes for the master bus and use minimum-phase modes on individual channels.

## FAQ

<!-- role:faq -->

Q: What is a multiband compressor and when should I use one?
A: A multiband compressor splits incoming audio into separate frequency bands and compresses each one on its own. This is useful when a single-band compressor causes problems it cannot fix on its own — for example, when heavy bass triggers gain reduction that also affects the high-mid frequencies, or when one narrow frequency range needs taming without touching the rest of the mix. If there is no interaction between frequency ranges and the dynamics issue is simple, a standard single-band compressor is usually the simpler and more suitable choice.

Q: Is TDR Nova a multiband compressor or a dynamic EQ?
A: It can work as both, and in practice the line between the two matters less than it once did. TDR Nova can be set up as a dynamic EQ, as a parallel compressor, or as a conventional multiband compressor, depending on how its bands are configured. It is built as a hybrid tool from the ground up, so any of these three uses are reasonable depending on what a given project calls for.

Q: Why is OTT so popular specifically in electronic music production?
A: OTT applies upward compression, which raises quiet parts of the signal, and downward compression, which reduces loud parts, at the same time across three frequency bands. This combination produces a dense, heavily compressed tonal quality that matches the forward, in-your-face sound common in modern electronic music. Because it costs nothing and takes very little time to learn, it is also simply quick to reach for when that specific sound is wanted.

Q: Can free multiband compressors be used on professional or commercial releases?
A: Yes, for most typical compression needs. TDR Nova and Waves C6 Lite both provide solid multiband control suitable for professional work. What paid tools tend to add is faster workflow, more advanced phase-processing options, and more detailed metering — not a basic difference in sound quality for ordinary use cases.

Q: What is the difference between a multiband compressor and a multiband limiter?
A: A compressor reduces gain above a set threshold at a chosen ratio, applied gradually. A limiter uses a very steep ratio, generally ten to one or steeper, to stop a signal from going above a set ceiling almost entirely. Some mastering tools combine both functions in one processing chain. In general, a multiband compressor is the right tool for shaping dynamics during mixing, while a limiter is applied afterward to control the final output level before a track is delivered.

Q: Which free multiband compressor works best for mastering?
A: TDR Nova stands out among free options for mastering use, since its linear-phase mode and mid/side processing address the phase accuracy and stereo control that mastering work typically demands. Waves C6 Lite is worth considering when six separate bands are needed, and a tool like MMultiband suits situations calling for finer per-band control. For a complete mastering chain that also covers limiting, stereo imaging, and reference metering in one package, a full paid mastering suite still goes beyond what any free standalone compressor offers.

Q: Do multiband compressors place a heavy load on a computer's processor?
A: It varies depending on how the plugin is built. Crossover-based multiband compressors running in linear-phase modes use more processing power, since they add latency and extra calculation for each band. It is worth checking the processing requirements listed for each plugin individually to understand what it will need. When processing power is limited, a practical approach is to use linear-phase modes only on the master bus and switch to minimum-phase modes on individual tracks.
