## Compression Parameters: What You Actually Need to Understand

Before any plugin discussion, four parameters are worth understanding at the level of cause and effect — because no interface makes compression intuitive if you do not know what you are adjusting. **Threshold** sets the level at which compression begins. Lower the threshold and the compressor engages more often. Raise it and only the loudest peaks get caught. This is the parameter most producers learn first and get right quickly. **Ratio** controls how aggressively the compressor responds once the signal crosses threshold. A 2:1 ratio is gentle and transparent. An 8:1 or higher ratio approaches limiting. A common starting range for mixing duties is 3:1 to 6:1. **Attack and release** are where compression character comes from. A slow attack on a snare lets the transient through before the compressor engages — that is how punchy drums happen. A fast attack on a bass guitar smooths out level differences before you hear them. Release determines how quickly compression stops working after the signal drops below threshold. Pumping and breathing artifacts are almost always release-time problems, not ratio problems. **Makeup gain** compensates for the volume lost during compression. Set it last, after the compressor is dialed in, so volume comparisons do not fool you into thinking a louder signal sounds better compressed. ---

## FAQ

<!-- role:faq -->

Q: What compression ratio works well for vocal mixing?
A: A typical range for lead vocals is 3:1 to 6:1, with attack times set between 5 and 20 milliseconds so the vocal's opening consonants stay clear. The exact ratio matters less than the attack time — setting the attack so the vocal's initial sounds get through before compression kicks in is what keeps articulation intact.

Q: What is mix bus compression, and when should it be used?
A: Mix bus compression is light compression applied to the full stereo mix before mastering. Its purpose is to add cohesion rather than to reduce dynamics heavily. A common approach is to aim for about 2 to 4 decibels of gain reduction using slow attack settings, so the compressor reacts to the overall energy of the mix instead of individual transient peaks. TDR Kotelnikov and the Waves SSL G-Master Buss Compressor are both suited to this kind of work.

Q: What is parallel compression, and how is it set up?
A: Parallel compression combines a heavily compressed copy of a signal with the original, uncompressed version. The compressed layer adds density and sustain, while the dry layer keeps the transient punch intact. It is often used on drum buses and individual drum tracks, with the compressed signal typically blended in somewhere between 20 and 50 percent, depending on how much extra density the material needs.

Q: Is FabFilter Pro-C 3 a good choice for a bedroom producer?
A: It can work well as both a learning tool and a production tool, largely because of its visual metering, which shows compression behavior as it happens rather than leaving it to guesswork. Its range of compression modes covers uses that would otherwise call for several separate purpose-built plugins, which can make it a practical single choice for a home studio.

Q: How can I tell if I am compressing too much?
A: For individual tracks, roughly 3 to 6 decibels of gain reduction is a typical, transparent working range, while bus and mix-bus compression usually stays closer to 2 to 4 decibels. Compression tends to become clearly audible once gain reduction reaches 6 to 10 decibels on most material. Whether that is a problem depends on the goal — it may be exactly the effect wanted, as with parallel compression or a deliberate creative effect, or it may be a sign of overdoing it if transparency was the aim.
