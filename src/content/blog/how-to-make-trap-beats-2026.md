## Step 2: Design the 808

The 808 is the defining element of trap. There are two common approaches: either sample and repitch a classic Roland TR-808 bass hit, or synthesize from scratch using a wavetable or subtractive synth. ## Step 5: Mix for Low-End Punch and Clarity

Trap mixes succeed or fail in the low end. The kick and 808 must occupy complementary frequency zones — the kick handling transient punch in the 50–80Hz range while the 808 sustains from 40–120Hz depending on the pitch of the note played. Frequency collision between the two is the most common technical problem in amateur trap mixes.

Sidechain compression between kick and 808 is a common technique in trap production. One controlled approach is transient-sensitive sidechain routing — optical or VCA emulations at medium attack (5–15ms) and medium release (100–250ms) — letting the 808 breathe after each kick hit without an audible pumping artifact. Ghost sidechain techniques, where a duplicated kick MIDI event triggers the compressor without being heard in the mix, give independent timing control over the ducking behavior.

For mix bus processing, high-pass filtering at 28–32Hz removes sub-rumble before limiting. Streaming loudness targets cluster around -14 LUFS integrated, though competitive trap masters frequently land at -8 to -10 LUFS short-term due to the genre's loudness expectations. True peak control at -1dBTP is standard before export. ## FAQ

<!-- role:faq -->

Q: What DAW do most trap producers use?
A: There is no single DAW required for trap production. FL Studio, Ableton Live, and Logic Pro X are all common choices. FL Studio's step sequencer and pattern-based workflow suit hi-hat programming and loop arrangement well.

Q: Do I need to sample to make trap beats?
A: No. Sampling — taking elements from soul, jazz, or R&B records — is one approach with a long history in hip-hop, but building a beat entirely from synthesized sounds is equally valid and avoids the complications of clearing samples. Synthesizer plugins can produce fully synthesized melodic leads that sound close enough to sampled material that the distinction is largely academic.

Q: Why does my 808 disappear on phone speakers?
A: A plain sine-wave 808 carries almost no content above its fundamental frequency. Without upper harmonics, it does not translate well to small speakers with limited low-frequency response, such as phone speakers. Adding harmonic saturation on a parallel channel — even something as simple as a gentle soft clip or tape-style saturation — introduces higher-frequency content that survives mono summing and comes through on smaller playback systems.

Q: What BPM should trap beats be?
A: Trap beats typically fall between 130 and 145 beats per minute, with hi-hats usually programmed at double time, creating the effect of sixteenth-note rolls running at roughly 260 to 290 beats per minute.

Q: How loud should a trap beat be before sending to artists?
A: For rough instrumentals, common practice is to sit around -6 to -9 LUFS integrated, leaving headroom for mastering. Fully mastered trap aimed at streaming platforms generally lands between -8 and -14 LUFS integrated, depending on the platform's normalization settings — Spotify normalizes to -14 LUFS, so louder masters get turned down anyway.
